# Figure Type Guide

Use this reference to choose the safest and most useful figure type.

## Graphical abstract

Use when the paper has a clear study arc:
- problem or context
- intervention or method
- main finding
- implication

Best for:
- translational studies
- multi-step biological stories
- papers that need a one-glance summary

Avoid when:
- the paper only contributes one small technical point
- the source lacks enough structure to support a narrative

## Mechanism diagram

Use when the paper describes:
- a pathway
- causal relationships
- activation or inhibition
- cell-cell interactions
- molecular or systems-level logic

Best for:
- signaling papers
- immunology, cancer biology, neuroscience, metabolism
- intervention-to-effect stories

Avoid when:
- the paper only shows correlation
- the mechanism is speculative and not presented as established

## Workflow or methods figure

Use when the main contribution is procedural:
- cohort assembly
- sequencing and analysis pipelines
- screening workflows
- engineering or computational methods

Best for:
- methods papers
- benchmark studies
- data-processing or instrument workflows

Avoid when:
- the user actually wants a results summary instead of a pipeline

## Quantitative summary figure

Use only when the source supports real measurements or robust directional comparison.

Best for:
- side-by-side result summaries
- simple comparative panels
- data-backed takeaway charts

Never use if:
- you do not have numbers
- you only know that one thing is "higher" without stable context
- the paper text does not justify a precise chart form

## Cover-style illustration

Use when the user wants:
- a journal-cover feel
- a stylized hero image
- a visually striking research illustration

Best for:
- outreach
- talks
- title slides
- posters

Constraint:
- keep the central scientific motif faithful even if the style is dramatic
